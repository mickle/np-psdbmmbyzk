Reading the cases

The Base case earns +$0.56M (+9.9% on cost), below the customary 15–20% spec margin. The Best case clears it at +35.8% (+$1.77M). The Worst case loses $0.71M (−10.8%). Owner cash is ≈$1.04M–$1.44M over about 27 months, of which $0.67M–$0.98M is the 30% equity.

What has to be true
  1. A sale at ≈$1,450/sf or more. In the comp set only 323 Cordova ($1,449/sf) and 1695 Brightwaters ($1,500/sf) reached that level, both with direct water access and docks. The comp median for homes of 6,000 sf or less (n = 5) is $1,347/sf (1800 N Shore, same street); the Worst case sits at the median, Base 8% and Best 15% above it, and the Best case is 3% above the highest sale (§5).
  2. Hard cost of $350–450/sf for the building. This is at or below the low end of published builder ranges ($425–750/sf for flood-zone and waterfront work, Appendix D). Pool, hardscape and landscape ($160K) and site work ($75K) are separate. The owner’s team manages the build, so a full GC fee (10–20%) is replaced by a 2% qualifier fee.
  3. The seller subordinates the note and defers its interest to the sale. The construction lender has to accept a deferred-interest note behind its loan. Without subordination, ≈$1M+ more cash is needed up front.
  4. Timing. Each month of delay during the build or sale costs ≈$13–39K (cash carry ≈$13–26K/mo plus $0–12.5K/mo of accruing note interest; $6.25K at Base 3%), and buyer depth above $6.5M is thin.

Top levers: savings vs. the $450/sf Base budget (ranges overlap; not additive)

  1. Spec-level finishes with targeted splurges: ≈$150–220K. Spend in the kitchen, primary bath, entry and the view wall; standardize everything else (cabinets, counters, appliances, flooring, tile, trim, fixtures, lighting).
  2. Simple massing and a value-engineered plan: ≈$70–140K. Rectangular footprint, minimal corners, one roof form, stacked plumbing, standard spans; a repeat or design-build plan reviewed for cost before permit.
  3. Direct trade buying and competitive bids: ≈$60–120K. Three or more bids per trade; buy appliances, tile, fixtures, lighting and windows direct at trade pricing.
  4. Glass strategy: ≈$40–90K. Hold openings to ≈1,100 sf in standard sizes; 8–12 ft sliders instead of pivot or multi-slide pocket walls; value-tier impact line.
  5. Foundation choice for the 8 ft raise: ≈$30–80K. Geotech first: spread footings on compacted fill vs. piles; CMU (concrete masonry unit, i.e. concrete block) ground level with garage vs. structural columns; precast or filigree elevated deck vs. cast-in-place.

Owner management already replaces a 10–20% GC fee with the 2% qualifier fee in the pro forma; the remaining saving here is leaner general conditions (≈$25–50K). Skipping the elevator saves ≈$35–45K but is not recommended (Appendix M).

Reality check
  • Local ranges. Tampa Bay production builders run ≈$180–260/sf and semi-custom ≈$250–350/sf (Toll Brothers ≈$310–380) [N6, N7]; luxury custom runs $425–750+/sf, with coastal and flood-zone work 15–25% higher [N7]. St. Pete flood-zone custom builders quote $425–525/sf (FEMA flood zone AE) [S1]; A2 Construction quotes $320–520/sf for elevated waterfront, before the GC fee [S3]. City permit declarations for nearby new homes run $177–347/sf, a floor rather than a budget (Appendix D).
  • Verdict. $350/sf is reachable only as a disciplined, owner-managed, semi-custom build: simple massing, standard glass and spec-level finishes with a few splurges. That is below what the comps that set $1,450–1,550/sf were built to, and the main risk is the sale price, not the build.
  • What the miss costs. At $350/sf hard, net profit is +$1.60M (+31.3%) at $1,550/sf, +$1.17M (+22.8%) at $1,450/sf, +$0.73M (+14.4%) at $1,350/sf and +$0.30M (+5.9%) at $1,250/sf. A $400/sf build selling at $1,450/sf earns +$0.87M (+16.1%). (Base 3% note; 2% qualifier fee; contingency spent 1% of the build budget at $350/sf, 3% at $400/sf.)
  • Finish floor at this price. Buyers at $6M+ expect an elevator, impact glass with large sliders to the view, 10 ft+ ceilings, a designer kitchen with a pro-style or integrated appliance suite and stone counters, a spa-style primary bath, one premium floor material throughout, a whole-house generator and a pool with outdoor kitchen (judgment based on the comp set in §5).

Key negotiation point: subordination. A construction lender requires first lien [S32, S36]. Keeping the seller note in place through the build requires the seller to subordinate the note to the construction lender (recorded subordination agreement), or a lender willing to sit behind it in second position, which few lenders accept [S36]. A subordinated seller risks being wiped out in a foreclosure, so expect the seller to ask for something in return (a higher rate, a shorter term, personal guarantee or a price adjustment). If the seller will not subordinate, the fallback is a construction loan that pays off the note at closing, which requires a lender that finances land plus vertical and roughly $1M+ more owner cash up front.

What defines the top of this market

  1. Water access sets the $/sf ceiling. Every sale above $1,350/sf has direct open-bay or harbor frontage with a dock (1695 Brightwaters, 323 Cordova) or extensive deeded water lots with a dock (1800 N Shore). View-only and across-the-street water lots trade at ≈$1,150–1,180/sf.
  2. Size drives the $7M+ prices. $7M+ sales are 5,900–7,600 sf at $1,150–1,500/sf; 4,500–4,700 sf homes top out at $6.1–6.75M.
  3. Newer is required, style is not. Winning designs range from modern (323 Cordova) to coastal vernacular (1695 Brightwaters) to West Indies (2030 Coffee Pot) and restored Palm Beach (1800 N Shore). The common thread is CBS/block-and-stucco construction, a premium roof (tile or standing-seam metal), and large glazing toward the water.
  4. Outdoor living is standard. Saltwater or infinity pools with heaters, outdoor kitchens, covered terraces and courtyards appear in all six; the seller’s courtyard-and-pool concept fits this pattern.
  5. Elevation is expected. Recent builds sit on raised floors with grand entry stairs; the ≈8 ft raise at 1500 N Shore is in line with the market, not a handicap.
  6. Soft spots: large price cuts at 105 Cordova and 337 Coffee Pot show depth is thin above $7M.
L. Risks, timeline and open questions

Key risks and diligence

  • Subordination: the base case depends on the seller subordinating the note through the sale. Without it, the construction loan must retire the note, roughly $1M+ more cash is needed, and few spec lenders finance land plus vertical at this size.
  • Spec financing: combined debt (note + loan) is ≈58–84% of value across the cases, against 65–75% completed-value caps; a first-time spec borrower should expect 65–75% LTC and private pricing (≈10–11.5%).
  • Note term: the note must run at least 27–33 months (design, build, sale), with extension rights if the sale is slow.
  • Survey and trees: order topo, elevations and a tree survey; confirm the VE line, the 15 ft alley and the 14,160 vs. 14,553 sf discrepancy. Arborist report on the NE oak and any other protected trees.
  • Zoning determinations: front façade and entry (15th Ave), pool placement, alley rear setback, impervious treatment of the courtyard and pool.
  • Flood and insurance: confirm freeboard and datum; obtain builder’s risk, flood and wind quotes; buyers will underwrite the completed-home flood/wind premium [S19].
  • Build cost: builder-published ranges, not bids; obtain 2–3 trade-level budgets on a schematic and a signed qualifier agreement.
  • Market / absorption: the buyer pool above $5M is thin (price cuts at 105 Cordova and 337 Coffee Pot); each extra month on market costs ≈$26–33K at Base, including $6.25K of accruing note interest at 3%.
  • Licensing: a spec build requires a licensed general contractor (owner-builder status is not available for a home built for sale, Fla. Stat. §489.103(7)); the model carries a 2% qualifier fee, and the qualifier must genuinely supervise the work (§489.1195) [S44].
